Ramyeon-sinusoidal function

A Sinusoidal function

Choice of Image & Parent Function:
I selected a photograph of ramyeon noodles because I frequently eat them for lunch near the Sunway convenience store. To find a real-world object to model, I observed small details around the campus. I noticed that the repetitive, wavy patterns of the noodles look remarkably similar to a periodic wave. Therefore, I chose the standard sine function, y=sin(x), as my parent function to capture this continuous, undulating shape.

Equation and Order of Transformations:
To precisely align the parent function with the actual flow, curves, and position of the noodle in the photograph, I applied transformations in the following mathematical order.

Vertical Stretch (Amplitude): The standard sine graph only oscillates between 1 and -1. Because the actual ripples of the noodle are much taller, I increased the amplitude by multiplying the function by a factor of 1.77.

Horizontal Reflection: To match the starting direction and the 'ups and downs' of the wave perfectly with the photo, I multiplied the x-coefficient by -1. This reflects the graph horizontally across the y-axis.

Horizontal Phase Shift: I translated the graph 0.9 units to the right to align the starting point of the wave with the visible noodle.

Vertical Translation: I shifted the entire graph 4.7 units down to position it correctly over the image axes.

Final Transformed Equation:
y = 1.77sin(-(x - 0.9)) - 4.7

Domain and Range Analysis:
Domain: D = {x∈ℝ│2 ≤ x ≤ 15}
I restricted the model strictly to the interval 2 ≤ x ≤ 15 because this represents the exact section of the noodle that is visible in the photograph. Outside of these endpoints, the graph would extend into empty space where no noodles are shown, rendering the model unrealistic.

Range: R = {y∈ℝ│-6.47 ≤ y ≤ -2.93}
Since a sinusoidal function is continuous and oscillates symmetrically around its midline, its behavior and range are determined entirely by its vertical parameters rather than its domain endpoints. The vertical translation shifts the midline of the wave down to y = -4.7. The vertical stretch extends the peaks and troughs of the wave 1.77 units above and below this midline. Therefore, the minimum value is calculated as -4.7 - 1.77 = -6.47, and the maximum value is -4.7 + 1.77 = -2.93.

Comparison with Parent Function:
Compared to the parent function y=sin(x)—which has an amplitude of 1, a period of 2π, a midline at y=0, and passes through the origin (0,0) moving upwards—this transformed model is vertically stretched, flows in the opposite horizontal direction due to the y-axis reflection, and has been shifted both horizontally and vertically into the fourth quadrant to accurately mimic the physical structure of the ramyeon.

Transformation:

Summary of Transformation:
y = secx

Outside of this interval, the graph would create repeated branches and vertical asymptotes that do not exist on the real sculpture.

Curve of the Sunway Collage Building Arch as a Function of the Horizontal Distance from the Centre

Polynomial Function

Parent function: y =x^2

Transformation:
I chose this photograph because the curved shape of the Sunway College building arch closely resembled a quadratic function. To model the building arch appearing in the image, I began by identifying that its shape was similar to the parent quadratic function y=x^2as a starting point. From observing the orientation of the curve in my image, I noticed that the parabola opened downward rather than upward. This indicated a reflection across the x-axis. Therefore, I modified the parent function to y=-x^2 to match the direction of the arch in the photograph.

Initially, I found it difficult to determine the correct vertical stretch or compression factor because the graph was positioned over a real-life image rather than directly on the coordinate plane. To address this, I first focused on matching the curvature of the arch without considering translations. By comparing the width and steepness of the parabola to the shape of the building arch, I determined that a vertical compression was needed rather than a stretch.

Through observation and refinement, I found that a compression factor of 0.2produced a curve that closely matched the arch in the image. Since the vertex of the parabola already aligned closely with the origin, no horizontal or vertical translations were required. This resulted in the function y=-0.2x^2 which closely models the shape of the Sunway College building arch shown in the photograph.

Summary of transformation:
* Parent Function: y=x^2

* Vertical compression by a factor of 0.2:-0.2x^2

* No horizontal translation: h=0

*No vertical translation: k=0

Domain: {𝑥∈ℝ∣-1.35≤x≤1.35}
I restricted the domain to the visible portion of the building arch shown in the photograph. Although the quadratic function continues infinitely in both directions, only a small section of the parabola appears in the image. Therefore, the mathematical model is only valid for the visible section of the building arch.

Range: {y∈ℝ∣-0.36≤y≤0}
The parent function y=x^2 has a domain of D={x∈ℝ} and a range of R={y∈ℝ∣y≥0 }.This means the function is defined for all real x-values and produces only non-negative outputs. The parent parabola opens upward and has a minimum point at the vertex (0ⓜ,0).

After applying transformations, my function became a downward-opening parabola because of the reflection across the x-axis. The graph remains continuous because quadratic functions do not contain discontinuities or asymptotes. The transformed graph has a maximum point at (0,0) and an axis of symmetry at x=0.

Since the function is continuous on the restricted interval, the range can be determined by evaluating the endpoints of the domain. By substituting x=1.35 and x=1.3into the function, I obtained approximate y-values between -0.36 and 0. Therefore, the range of the function is approximately R={y∈ℝ∣-0.36≤y≤0 }.The transformed function differs from the parent function because its orientation has been reflected across the x-axis and its steepness has been reduced through a vertical compression. These transformations allowed the parabola to more accurately model the real-life curve of the Sunway College building arch in the photograph.

Exponential Model of a Modern chair

f(x)=2^x

Parent function: y = 2^x

Transformation:
I chose this photograph of a modern chair at Sunway College because its design is a striking example of how mathematical principles are integrated into functional furniture. The backrest doesn't just curve randomly; it follows a clear exponential growth pattern—starting relatively flat at the seat and then rising sharply. This visual 'acceleration' in the design is what made it a perfect candidate for an exponential model.  I chose the parent function y = a^x. To align the function with the chair's specific design, I set the base to 1.9, which determines the steepness of the curve. Then, I applied a horizontal translation of 0.3 units to the right (h = 0.3) and a vertical translation of 1 unit up (k = 1). These transformations were necessary to position the curve precisely over the chair's backrest in the coordinate plane.  

Summary of transformation:
* Parent Function: y = 1.9x(using 1.9 as the base)

* Horizontal Translation: h = 0.3 (shifts the graph 0.3 units to the right)

* Vertical Translation: k = 1 (shifts the graph 1 unit up)

* Final Transformed Equation: y = 1.9^(x-0.3) + 1

Domain and Range Analysis:
Domain: {x | x ∈ ℝ, -5 ≤ x ≤ 2.5}
• I restricted the domain to this interval because it represents the actual horizontal span of the chair's backrest captured in the photograph. Beyond these points, the function would extend indefinitely, which would no longer accurately reflect the physical dimensions and structural limits of the object.

Range: {y | y ∈ ℝ, 1.1 ≤ y ≤ 7.5}
The range is bounded by the height of the chair. The lower bound starts just above the horizontal asymptote at y = 1, and the upper bound reaches 7.5 at the highest point of the backrest. This range effectively quantifies the vertical space occupied by the chair's curvature relative to the floor. .

Parent function: y = log(x)

Transformation:
For this part of my project, I photographed an escalator handrail at the mall. I noticed that the curve where the escalator transitions from its steep incline to the flat landing area perfectly models a logarithmic growth pattern. I started with the parent function y = log(x). To match the specific curve of the railing, I applied a vertical stretch with a factor of a = 6.3. I then used a horizontal translation of 2.5 units to the left (h = -2.5) and a vertical translation of 4.4 units up (k = 4.4). These transformations allowed the mathematical model to align precisely with the physical curvature of the escalator's glass support and handrail.

Summary of transformation:
* Vertical Stretch: a = 6.3

* Horizontal Translation: h = -2.5 (Shifted 2.5 units left)

* Vertical Translation: k = 4.4 (Shifted 4.4 units up)

* Final Transformed Equation: y = 6.3 log(x + 2.5) + 4.4

Domain and Range Analysis:
Domain: [-2.4,0.5]
I have strictly restricted the domain to this interval to match the specific section of the escalator railing captured in my photograph. The lower bound of -2.4 is chosen to be just to the right of the vertical asymptote (x = -2.5), where the curve begins its sharp ascent. The upper bound of 0.5 limits the function to the point where the handrail begins to flatten out significantly. This ensures the mathematical model is only active over the physical object.

Range: [-7,8]
Within the specified domain, the function spans a vertical distance from -8 up to 7. These values represent the vertical height of the railing relative to my coordinate system's origin. The range effectively captures the total rise of the curve as it moves from the lower landing toward the upper section of the escalator shown in the frame..
